# When the River Comes Back
## In Vermont, federal mitigation funding tracks past flood damage more closely than future flood risk. 

---

[PHOTO: Aerial or street-level view of Bennington, with the Walloomsac River visible]

The Walloomsac River cuts through downtown Bennington past old mill buildings, parking lots, rail crossings, and neighborhoods that have flooded repeatedly over the years. During heavy rain, water backs into the same low-lying areas again and again. By several federal flood-risk measures, Bennington ranks among the most exposed communities in Vermont.

It has never received federal Hazard Mitigation Assistance funding for flood projects.

Nearly 200 miles north, Newport City sits along the southern edge of Lake Memphremagog in Vermont's Northeast Kingdom. The geography is different, but the vulnerability is familiar: aging infrastructure, economic strain, limited municipal capacity, and neighborhoods exposed to flood risk. Newport ranks near the top of the state in overall flood vulnerability. It has also received no federal mitigation funding.

The two communities are very different places. But they arrive at the same point in the data.

Across Vermont, the pattern repeats.

More than half of Vermont municipalities have never received funding through FEMA's primary flood mitigation grant programs. Some are small mountain towns with only a few hundred residents. Others, like Bennington, are among the largest communities in the state. Some have flooded repeatedly. Others sit in watersheds that climate models suggest are becoming more dangerous over time.

The question is not simply which towns flood. Vermont floods regularly. The deeper question is how institutions decide which communities count as priorities before the next disaster arrives.

That question turns out to depend heavily on how risk itself gets measured.

## The Geography of Risk

[PHOTO: Aerial damage from Hurricane Irene, 2011 — Vermont road washout or bridge collapse]

On August 28, 2011, Hurricane Irene stalled over Vermont and triggered the most destructive natural disaster in the state's modern history. Rivers overtopped banks across all fourteen counties. Roads washed away. Bridges collapsed. Entire downtowns flooded. Damages approached [three-quarters of a billion dollars](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/26/vermont-flood-anniversaries-once-brought-revelry-and-relief-this-summer-will-be-different/).

Recovery took years. Federal money flowed into rebuilding roads, repairing public infrastructure, and buying out some of the most flood-prone properties in the state.

Twelve years later, Vermont flooded again.

[PHOTO: 2023 flooding in Montpelier or Barre — downtown street with water]

In July 2023, extreme rainfall pushed rivers across central Vermont over their banks for the second time in little more than a decade. Montpelier flooded again. Barre's downtown filled with water. Damage estimates once again climbed into the hundreds of millions of dollars [[over $600 million in property](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/26/vermont-flood-anniversaries-once-brought-revelry-and-relief-this-summer-will-be-different/)].

By the second statewide flood disaster in little more than a decade, the phrase “once-in-a-generation” had started to lose meaning. 

I grew up in Vermont and have watched flooding shift from a periodic disaster to a recurring feature of life across the state. After Irene, "Vermont Strong" became a shorthand for resilience and rebuilding, a mark of pride. That wasn’t the case after 2023. “Vermont Strong” was gone. This wasn’t a one-off. It was a long-term condition.

Climate researchers have warned for years that extreme rainfall events in the Northeast are becoming more frequent and more intense. Vermont's geography amplifies the consequences: steep terrain, narrow river valleys, aging culverts, undersized bridges, and development patterns built around rivers that once seemed manageable.

After disasters, the federal government mobilizes quickly. FEMA issues emergency declarations. Infrastructure repair money arrives. Roads reopen.

Mitigation funding operates differently.

FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Assistance (HMA) programs are meant to break the disaster cycle — not to repair what the last flood destroyed, but to prevent the next flood from destroying it in the first place. It pays for home buyouts that permanently remove families from floodplains. It funds culvert replacements, stormwater systems, early warning networks, and flood-proofing for public buildings. Since 1990, FEMA has obligated more than [$30 billion](data) in mitigation grants across the country.

But in Vermont, those resources are unevenly distributed.

More than 52 percent of Vermont municipalities have never received flood mitigation funding through the program.

[GRAPHIC: "52%" — large stat callout or donut chart showing share of Vermont towns with zero HMA funding]

At first glance, that might simply look like a reflection of geography. Some towns flood more than others. Some communities are larger. Some face greater exposure.

But the distribution of funding raises a deeper institutional question: what exactly counts as "risk" inside the systems that allocate mitigation money?

## Measuring Need

[GRAPHIC: Index schematic — "Need = Risk (EAL) + Vulnerability (poverty rate, elderly share, no-vehicle households)"]

To explore that question, I built a simple statewide flood mitigation need index for Vermont municipalities.

The model combines two components.

The first is physical flood risk, measured using FEMA's Expected Annual Loss estimates — a nationwide model estimating average annual flood damage exposure based on hazard intensity and the value of exposed assets.

The second is social vulnerability: poverty rates, elderly population share, and households without vehicle access, all drawn from Census data.

The logic is straightforward. Flood exposure alone does not fully describe community risk. A wealthy town and a low-income town facing similar flood hazards do not experience disasters in the same way. Recovery capacity matters. Mobility matters. Local administrative capacity matters.

Both components were normalized using rank-based percentiles so towns could be compared relative to one another rather than by raw dollar totals. Burlington will almost always produce higher raw damage estimates than a small rural town simply because it is larger. Relative ranking allows smaller communities with high vulnerability to remain visible inside the analysis.

The result is intentionally lightweight compared to the kinds of models produced by large agencies or research institutions. This is not a hydrodynamic simulation or parcel-level engineering study. It is a prototype — both for this project and for the kind of climate-risk analysis I hope to continue building.

But one of the more interesting findings is that relatively simple composite models can still identify broad structural patterns surprisingly well.

That matters because policymaking rarely happens under conditions of perfect information. The practical question is often not whether a model captures every variable with precision, but whether it captures enough of reality to support better decisions.

How much precision do policymakers actually need before action becomes possible?

Even at this scale, the broader patterns emerge clearly.

[GRAPHIC: Choropleth map — need index by Vermont town, diverging palette, high-need towns labeled including Bennington and Newport]

The geography of need follows recognizable patterns. High-need communities cluster along major river corridors: the Connecticut River valley, the Winooski watershed, the White River basin, and the Battenkill and Walloomsac drainages in southern Vermont. The Northeast Kingdom appears repeatedly as well, driven less by large population centers than by overlapping vulnerability factors: poverty, aging populations, and weaker municipal capacity.

Bennington and Newport appear near the top of the rankings for different reasons. Bennington scores high on physical exposure and infrastructure risk. Newport scores especially high on vulnerability. Both rank near the top statewide in overall need.

Neither has received mitigation funding.

One important caveat: this model is a comparative tool, not a verdict. It estimates relative need across Vermont towns. It does not prove that any individual funding decision was wrong or that any specific community was deliberately overlooked.

The useful signal appears in the aggregate.

## What the Data Shows

[GRAPHIC: Choropleth map — HMA funding received by Vermont town, same projection and scale as above]

The funding map looks different from the need map.

Funding is not randomly distributed. Large clusters appear around areas heavily affected by Irene and the 2023 floods. Communities that entered the federal disaster system — and learned how to navigate it — often appear repeatedly across multiple funding cycles.

But the overlap between measured need and actual mitigation funding is weaker than expected for a system intended to prioritize future risk reduction.

[GRAPHIC: Scatter plot — need rank (x-axis) vs. funding rank (y-axis), quadrant lines, annotated outliers including Bennington and Newport labeled as notable zero-funding cases]

Each point represents a Vermont municipality. The x-axis shows flood mitigation need rank. The y-axis shows total mitigation funding rank.

If funding consistently followed measured need, the points would roughly organize into a diagonal line. High-need communities would also tend to be highly funded.

Instead, the relationship is weak. Depending on model specification, the statistical correlation between need and funding ranges from roughly 0.10 to 0.30.

A different variable performs much better: prior flood insurance claims.

When compared against National Flood Insurance Program claims data, mitigation funding shows a substantially stronger relationship — roughly 0.55 in some model runs.

In practice, towns that have already experienced insured flood losses are significantly more likely to receive mitigation funding later.

That distinction matters because it changes the institutional meaning of risk.

There are at least two ways to think about flood mitigation.

One approach treats mitigation as a forward-looking system designed to reduce future vulnerability before disasters happen.

The other treats mitigation as a recovery-adjacent process that activates after communities have already suffered major losses and entered federal disaster systems.

The current funding geography in Vermont appears much closer to the second model.

That does not necessarily reflect bad individual decisions. FEMA's mitigation programs are structurally tied to disaster declarations, claims histories, and benefit-cost calculations that rely heavily on documented prior damage.

Institutions measure what they can see.

And documented disaster history is easier to quantify than future vulnerability.

This becomes especially important for communities like Newport and Bennington. Both rank highly under broader measures of future need. But neither has developed the same historical funding relationship with the federal mitigation system as towns repeatedly hit by declared disasters.

The result is a kind of institutional path dependency. Once towns enter the mitigation pipeline, they become more likely to remain inside it.

Communities outside the system face a much steeper climb.

[GRAPHIC: Zero-funding towns by need quintile]

The imbalance becomes clearest among zero-funding towns. High-need municipalities are disproportionately represented among communities that have never received mitigation support at all.

Many share similar characteristics:

- smaller administrative staffs
- lower median incomes
- older populations
- weaker grant-writing capacity
- limited prior engagement with federal mitigation programs

None of those factors reduce flood exposure.

But they do shape access to the systems designed to manage it.

## Why the Pattern Persists

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA funding obligated by year, showing spikes around 2011–2022 and 2023+, with Irene and 2023 events labeled]

The structure of federal mitigation funding helps explain why these patterns persist over time.

Most large pools of Hazard Mitigation Assistance funding become available only after presidential disaster declarations. Communities hit hardest by recent disasters are therefore best positioned to apply for future mitigation money.

This creates a feedback loop.

A town floods, receives a disaster declaration, develops relationships with state and federal emergency management systems, hires consultants, learns the grant process, and eventually receives mitigation funding. When the next disaster arrives, that same town is far better prepared institutionally to apply again.

Relationships built during one disaster cycle persist into the next.

First-time applicants face a cold start.

Administrative capacity matters more than many public discussions of climate adaptation acknowledge. FEMA mitigation grants require engineering studies, environmental reviews, cost-benefit analyses, local matching funds, and lengthy applications. For a Vermont town run by a part-time administrator and a volunteer selectboard, that process can become a major barrier on its own.

The geography of institutional capacity starts to matter almost as much as the geography of flood risk itself.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA project types by share of total funding, showing dominance of acquisition/buyouts]

The composition of mitigation spending also reveals something important about how adaptation currently functions in practice.

One of the largest categories of Vermont mitigation funding is property acquisition and buyouts: purchasing flood-prone homes and removing them from the floodplain permanently.

From a purely economic perspective, buyouts often make sense. FEMA and independent researchers have repeatedly found them cost-effective over long time horizons.

But they also reveal a harder reality embedded within climate adaptation policy.

In many places, **the primary mechanism for reducing flood losses is not protection. It is relocation**.

That process unfolds unevenly. Communities that have already experienced repeated disasters are more likely to access buyout programs. Communities still outside the mitigation system often remain exposed until a sufficiently damaging event pushes them into it.

In other words, disaster experience itself becomes part of the eligibility pathway.

## What Happens Next

[GRAPHIC: Quadrant chart — need rank vs. funding rank scatter with quadrant overlays; Bennington and Newport labeled; other notable high-need, zero-funding towns visible]

Vermont is in the middle of redrawing its Flood Insurance Rate Maps, the official federal designations that define which areas face high flood risk. The remapping is long overdue; the current maps in many areas date back to the 70's or 80's and do not reflect the changed hydrological reality of a warming, wetter climate. When the new maps are finalized, some towns will find themselves in higher-risk designations, a change that affects insurance requirements, property values, and eligibility for state programs including Vermont's Emergency Relief and Assistance Fund, covering up to [[92.5% of disaster funding](https://floodtraining.vermont.gov/protection-tools/get-ready-new-fema-flood-insurance-rate-maps)].

At the same time, federal mitigation policy remains unstable. FEMA's BRIC program — Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities — was designed in part to address some of the equity and capacity gaps embedded within traditional mitigation systems by supporting disadvantaged communities and lowering barriers to entry. Its future has become politically uncertain.

The broader challenge extends beyond Vermont.

Across the United States, climate adaptation systems are increasingly being asked to function proactively rather than reactively. That requires institutions capable of identifying future vulnerability before catastrophic losses occur.

The analysis here does not prescribe a single policy solution. But it does suggest that the way institutions define and measure risk has direct consequences for which communities receive protection.

And current systems still appear to reward documented past disaster experience more consistently than projected future vulnerability.

## A Note on the Data

This analysis is based on publicly available FEMA, Census, and National Flood Insurance Program data. The methodology was tested across multiple normalization methods and model specifications, and the broader patterns remained consistent.

Several limitations remain important:

- FEMA flood hazard mapping remains incomplete in parts of Vermont.
- NFIP participation rates in Vermont are extremely low, meaning claims data underrepresents actual flood losses.
- Municipal-level aggregation obscures neighborhood-level differences.
- Funding data captures approved grants, not unsuccessful applications or unmet demand.

The rankings presented here should be interpreted as approximate comparative signals rather than definitive scores.

## The River Comes Back

[PHOTO: Bennington streetscape or the Walloomsac River at current water level]

Floodwater does not arrive evenly across landscapes, and neither does protection.

Some Vermont communities already sit deep inside the federal mitigation system. Others remain largely outside it, despite high exposure to future flooding.

As climate risk accelerates, the distinction will matter more.

The next major storm will not begin from a blank slate. It will arrive in a landscape already shaped by decades of institutional decisions about how risk is measured, how funding flows, and which communities become visible to the systems designed to protect them.
