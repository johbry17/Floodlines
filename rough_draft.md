# When the River Comes Back
## Flood money flows toward past disasters. Millions of Vermont residents live in the path of the next one.

---

[PHOTO: Aerial or street-level view of Bennington, with the Walloomsac River visible]

The Walloomsac River runs through the center of Bennington, Vermont, as it has for centuries — past the old mills, under the railroad bridges, around the bend where the flood gauge sits. When it rises, and it does rise, the water doesn't care about county borders or municipal budgets or the fact that Bennington is, by multiple federal measures, one of the most flood-exposed communities in the state.

It also doesn't know that Bennington has never received a dollar in federal flood mitigation funding.

Four hundred miles north, along the southern shore of Lake Memphremagog in Vermont's Northeast Kingdom, Newport City faces a different version of the same problem. Its flood vulnerability score ranks in the top one percent of Vermont towns. More than a fifth of its residents live below the poverty line [VERIFY: exact poverty rate for Newport City from ACS]. Newport has also received zero federal mitigation funding.

Two towns. Different geographies, different demographics, different flood profiles. The same answer when you ask how much federal help they've received to prepare for the next flood: nothing.

This is not an anomaly. It is a pattern.

## The Question Nobody Asked Out Loud

After each disaster, the federal government mobilizes. FEMA issues disaster declarations. Emergency funds flow for debris removal and infrastructure repair. And separately, a quieter program called Hazard Mitigation Assistance begins its slower work.

HMA is the money meant to break the cycle — not to repair what the last flood destroyed, but to prevent the next flood from destroying it in the first place. It pays for home buyouts that permanently remove families from floodplains. It funds culvert replacements, stormwater systems, early warning networks, and flood-proofing for public buildings. Since 1990, FEMA has obligated more than [VERIFY: national HMA total obligated since 1990] in mitigation grants across the country.

In Vermont, that money has been concentrated in a relatively small number of towns.

More than half of Vermont's towns — 52 percent — have never received a single dollar in flood mitigation funding.

[GRAPHIC: "52%" — large stat callout or donut chart showing share of Vermont towns with zero HMA funding]

That number is worth sitting with. Not the towns that received a little. Not the towns that got less than they needed. The towns that received nothing. In a state that has been struck by two catastrophic flood events in twelve years, in a state currently redrawing its flood risk maps because the old ones no longer reflect reality, more than half of all municipalities have never participated in the program designed to protect them.

Bennington is one of them. So is Newport.

## How We Measured the Gap

To understand whether this distribution makes sense — whether the money is going where the need is greatest — you need a way to measure need independently of whether funding has already arrived.

This analysis builds a need index: a town-level estimate of flood mitigation need based on two components. The first is risk — specifically, FEMA's own estimate of Expected Annual Loss (EAL), which models how much flood damage a community can expect to absorb each year, on average, based on hazard intensity and asset exposure. The second is vulnerability — a composite of three socioeconomic indicators drawn from Census data: the share of residents below the poverty line, the share of elderly residents, and the share of households without access to a vehicle.

The logic is straightforward: a town's need for mitigation investment is a function of both how badly it could be hit and how hard that hit would fall on the people who live there. A wealthy suburb with significant flood exposure is different, in policy terms, from a low-income rural community facing the same hazard.

Both components were normalized using rank-based percentiles so that towns are measured relative to each other, not in absolute dollar terms — a method that is robust to the enormous variation in community size across Vermont's 250-plus municipalities. Burlington's raw EAL will always dwarf a small agricultural town's; the question is where each town sits in the statewide distribution.

The framework was tested across more than twenty model specifications and two normalization methods. The results are consistent: the rankings are stable, the patterns are clear.

[GRAPHIC: Index schematic — "Need = Risk (EAL) + Vulnerability (poverty rate, elderly share, no-vehicle households)"]

One important caveat before the numbers: this is a relative ranking, not a verdict. It estimates where need is greatest across Vermont towns; it does not certify that any specific town has been wronged, or that any specific allocation decision was incorrect. Individual town ranks should be read as approximate. The patterns across the full distribution are where the signal is clearest.

## What the Maps Show

[GRAPHIC: Choropleth map — need index by Vermont town, diverging palette, high-need towns labeled including Bennington and Newport]

The need index map has a recognizable shape. High-need towns cluster in the river valleys — the Connecticut River corridor along the New Hampshire border, the Winooski watershed in central Vermont, the Battenkill and Walloomsac drainages in the southwest. The Northeast Kingdom shows up repeatedly, driven by vulnerability: high poverty rates, older populations, limited transportation options. These are the places that flood, and where flooding hits hardest.

[GRAPHIC: Choropleth map — HMA funding received by Vermont town, same projection and scale as above]

The funding map looks different.

It is not that funded towns are distributed randomly. The 2011 and 2023 floods left visible marks — towns that were federally declared disasters and subsequently navigated the grant process tend to cluster around those events. But the overlap between high-need and high-funded is weaker than you would expect from a program designed to direct resources toward risk.

[GRAPHIC: Scatter plot — need rank (x-axis) vs. funding rank (y-axis), quadrant lines, annotated outliers including Bennington and Newport labeled as notable zero-funding cases]

The scatter plot makes the relationship visible in a different way. Each dot is a Vermont town. The x-axis is its need rank; the y-axis is its funding rank. If the system were allocating resources according to need, you would expect a diagonal line — high need, high funding. Instead, the cloud is mostly flat. The statistical correlation between need and funding runs between 0.10 and 0.30 depending on the model specification. That is a weak relationship by any standard.

Compare that to a different variable: NFIP insurance claims. The correlation between past claims paid and HMA funding received is approximately 0.55. In plain terms: the best single predictor of whether a Vermont town gets mitigation money is whether it has already filed flood insurance claims.

The system is not anticipating the future. It is reimbursing the past.

## Why the Money Flows the Way It Does

This is not a story about fraud or negligence. It is a story about structure.

Federal mitigation funding is, by design, heavily tied to disaster declarations. HMA grants become available in large quantities after a major disaster is declared, which means towns that were hit — and hit hard enough to generate a federal declaration — are positioned to apply. Towns that haven't yet experienced a declared disaster are not.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA funding obligated by year, showing spikes around 2011–2022 and 2023+, with Irene and 2023 events labeled]

There is also the question of capacity. HMA grants are not automatic. They require applications. They require matching funds — typically 25 percent of project costs. They require environmental reviews, benefit-cost analyses, and project scoping. For a Vermont town with a part-time administrator and a volunteer select board, this is a significant barrier. Larger towns, towns that have been through the process before, towns with established relationships with state hazard mitigation officers — they have a structural advantage that compounds over time.

The data reflects this dynamic. Towns that received funding after Irene were significantly more likely to receive funding after the 2023 floods as well. Relationships built during one disaster cycle persist into the next. First-timers face a cold start.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA project types by share of total funding, showing dominance of acquisition/buyouts]

The project type breakdown reveals another dimension. The single largest category of HMA spending in Vermont is acquisition and buyouts — purchasing flood-prone properties and removing them from the floodplain permanently. This is considered one of the most cost-effective mitigation strategies available, and there is real evidence it works. But it also means that the primary mechanism for reducing flood losses is relocation: families and businesses permanently displaced from the places they've lived, often with limited options for where to go next. For a town that hasn't yet been through a disaster declaration, that process hasn't started.

The communities waiting for the first declaration are often the ones least equipped to survive it.

## Who Is Still Waiting

[GRAPHIC: Quadrant chart — need rank vs. funding rank scatter with quadrant overlays; Bennington and Newport labeled; other notable high-need, zero-funding towns visible]

Newport City, population 4,394, sits at the northern tip of Lake Memphremagog, a few miles from the Canadian border. It ranks in the 99th percentile of Vermont towns for vulnerability — driven by poverty rates, elderly population share, and limited transportation options that would leave many residents unable to evacuate quickly if they needed to. Its overall need score ranks in the 98th percentile statewide. It has received no HMA funding.

"Northeast Kingdom" is the phrase Vermonters use for this part of the state — a name that carries a certain pride in its remoteness and self-sufficiency, and also a long history of economic marginalization. In Bennington, the profile is different but the conclusion is the same. The Walloomsac River has a documented flood history. Bennington is the largest Vermont town on record with zero HMA funding.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — number of zero-funding towns by need quintile, showing disproportionate concentration of high-need towns in the zero-funding bucket]

Across Vermont, the pattern is consistent. High-need towns are disproportionately represented among the towns that have never received mitigation funding. Of the 18 towns in the top 30 percent of the need index with zero HMA funding, many share common characteristics: smaller populations, lower median incomes, locations in areas that experienced significant but not federally declared flooding, or simply no prior engagement with the grant system. None of those characteristics make them less exposed to the next flood. They just make it harder to access the money designed to help them prepare.

## What Could Change — and What's Getting Harder

Vermont is in the middle of redrawing its Flood Insurance Rate Maps — the official federal designations that define which areas face high flood risk. The remapping is long overdue; the current maps in many areas predate both Irene and the 2023 floods and do not reflect the changed hydrological reality of a warming, wetter climate. When the new maps are finalized, some towns will find themselves in higher-risk designations — a change that affects insurance requirements, property values, and eligibility for state programs including Vermont's Emergency Relief and Assistance Fund [VERIFY: ERAF eligibility criteria and connection to FIRM designations].

The maps are catching up to reality at a difficult moment.

In 2025, the federal government eliminated the Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities program — known as BRIC — which had been designed specifically to address the equity gaps in traditional HMA funding [VERIFY: exact details of BRIC elimination, date, and whether it was a full elimination or funding rescission]. BRIC offered lower match requirements, dedicated funding for disadvantaged communities, and technical assistance for towns without grant-writing capacity. It was imperfect and underfunded relative to the scale of the need. It was also the most direct federal acknowledgment that the existing system was not reaching the towns that needed it most.

Some states have moved to fill gaps like this through pre-positioned state mitigation funds and streamlined processes for small municipalities [VERIFY: specific examples of state-level programs, and Vermont's post-2023 state-level efforts if any exist]. The question is whether pace matches exposure.

The data does not prescribe a specific fix. What it does is show the shape of the problem: a system that responds to disasters rather than anticipating them, that rewards prior engagement over current need, and that leaves a significant share of vulnerable communities outside the funding stream — not through malice, but through structure.

## A Note on the Data

The findings here are robust to methodology — the patterns hold across model specifications, normalization methods, and variable choices. But several important limitations apply.

- FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer mapping is incomplete across Vermont; some towns have partial or outdated coverage, which may affect their risk estimates.
- NFIP participation in Vermont is extremely low — fewer than 2 percent of housing units carry flood insurance — meaning insurance claims data significantly underrepresents actual flood losses statewide.
- Town-level aggregation obscures within-town variation. A high need score for Bennington doesn't mean every neighborhood faces the same exposure.
- Funding data reflects approved grants, not applications submitted or assistance requested and denied. The gap between what was applied for and what was approved is not captured here.

Individual town rankings should be read as approximate signals, not precise verdicts. The patterns in aggregate are where the analysis has the most confidence.

## The River Doesn't Wait

[PHOTO: Bennington streetscape or the Walloomsac River at current water level]

Vermont's flood maps are being redrawn. The federal program most directly aimed at the equity gap has been eliminated. And somewhere on the Walloomsac, a gauge is reading the water level right now.

In Newport, a family in a low-lying neighborhood is making the same quiet calculation that flood-prone families make everywhere: whether to move, whether to stay, whether help is coming.

The data says the help is not flowing toward them. Not yet.

Whether that changes is a political question, not a technical one. But before it can change, the pattern has to be visible.

This is what it looks like.
