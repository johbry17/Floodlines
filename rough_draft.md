# When the River Comes Back
## In Vermont, federal mitigation funding tracks past flood damage more closely than future flood risk. 

---

[PHOTO: Aerial or street-level view of Bennington, with the Walloomsac River visible]

The Walloomsac River runs through downtown Bennington, past former mill buildings, rail crossings, and neighborhoods that have flooded repeatedly over the years. When heavy rain moves through southern Vermont, the river overtops the same low-lying areas again and again. By several federal flood-risk measures, Bennington ranks among the most exposed communities in the state. 

Despite that exposure, Bennington has never received a dollar in federal flood Hazard Mitigation Assistance funding.

Four hundred miles north, along the southern shore of Lake Memphremagog in Vermont's Northeast Kingdom, Newport City faces a different version of the same problem. Its flood vulnerability score ranks in the top one percent of Vermont towns. More than a fifth of its residents live below the poverty line ([23%](data)). Newport has also received zero federal mitigation funding.

The two towns have little in common geographically or economically. But they arrive at the same place in the data, the same answer when you ask how much federal help they've received to prepare for the next flood: nothing.

Across Vermont, the pattern repeats.

## The Floods They Remember

[PHOTO: Aerial damage from Hurricane Irene, 2011 — Vermont road washout or bridge collapse]

On August 28, 2011, Hurricane Irene moved up the eastern seaboard and stalled over Vermont. That storm became the most destructive natural disaster in the state's modern history. Rivers jumped their banks across all fourteen counties. Roads dissolved. Bridges collapsed. Seven Vermonters died. Entire downtowns in Waterbury, Brattleboro, and Rochester went underwater. The state estimated damages at [nearly $750 million](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/26/vermont-flood-anniversaries-once-brought-revelry-and-relief-this-summer-will-be-different/).

Recovery took years. Federal money eventually came, for infrastructure repairs, for buyouts of the most flood-prone properties, for the slow work of making communities more resilient.

Twelve years later, Vermont flooded again.

[PHOTO: 2023 flooding in Montpelier or Barre — downtown street with water]

In July 2023, two days of rain dropped up to nine inches on parts of central Vermont. The Winooski, the Lamoille, and the White River all surged. Montpelier, the state capital, flooded for the second time in twelve years. Barre lost its downtown. Final damage estimates ran into the hundreds of millions of dollars [[over $600 million in property](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/26/vermont-flood-anniversaries-once-brought-revelry-and-relief-this-summer-will-be-different/)].

By the second statewide flood disaster in little more than a decade, the phrase “once-in-a-generation” had started to lose meaning. 

I grew up in Vermont and have watched flooding become a more regular feature of life across the state over the past two decades. After Irene, “Vermont Strong” symbolized the resilience and rebuilding efforts, a mark of pride. That wasn’t the case after 2023. “Vermont Strong” was gone. This wasn’t a one-off. It was a long-term condition, akin to realizing you’d had a lifelong disease all along. [CONSIDER: Move to intro? Methodology? Mention twice my VT connection?]

## The Question Nobody Asked Out Loud

After each disaster, the federal government mobilizes. FEMA issues disaster declarations. Emergency funds flow for debris removal and infrastructure repair. And separately, a quieter program called Hazard Mitigation Assistance begins its slower work.

HMA is the money meant to break the disaster cycle — not to repair what the last flood destroyed, but to prevent the next flood from destroying it in the first place. It pays for home buyouts that permanently remove families from floodplains. It funds culvert replacements, stormwater systems, early warning networks, and flood-proofing for public buildings. Since 1990, FEMA has obligated more than [$30 billion](data) in mitigation grants across the country.

In Vermont, that money has been concentrated in a relatively small number of towns.

More than half of Vermont's towns — 52 percent — have never received a single dollar in flood mitigation funding through the program.

[GRAPHIC: "52%" — large stat callout or donut chart showing share of Vermont towns with zero HMA funding]

In a state that has been struck by two catastrophic flood disasters in twelve years, in a state currently redrawing its flood risk maps because the old ones no longer reflect reality, more than half of all municipalities have never participated in the program designed to protect them.

Bennington is one of them. So is Newport.

## How We Measured the Gap

To understand whether this distribution makes sense — whether the money is going where the need is greatest — you need a way to measure need independently of whether funding has already arrived.

This analysis builds a need index: a town-level estimate of flood mitigation need based on two components. The first is risk — specifically, FEMA's own estimate of Expected Annual Loss (EAL), which models how much flood damage a community can expect to absorb each year, on average, based on hazard intensity and asset exposure. The second is vulnerability — a composite of three socioeconomic indicators drawn from Census data: the share of residents below the poverty line, the share of elderly residents, and the share of households without access to a vehicle.

The logic is straightforward: a town's need for mitigation investment is a function of both how badly it could be hit and how hard that hit would fall on the people who live there. A wealthy suburb with significant flood exposure is different, in policy terms, from a low-income rural community facing the same hazard.

Both components were normalized using rank-based percentiles so that towns are measured relative to each other, not in absolute dollar terms — a method that is robust to the enormous variation in community size across Vermont's 250-plus municipalities. Burlington's raw EAL will always dwarf a small agricultural town's; the question is where each town sits in the statewide distribution.

The framework was tested across more than twenty model specifications and two normalization methods. The results are consistent: the rankings are stable, the patterns are clear.

[GRAPHIC: Index schematic — "Need = Risk (EAL) + Vulnerability (poverty rate, elderly share, no-vehicle households)"]

One important caveat before the numbers: this is a relative ranking, not a verdict. It estimates where need is greatest across Vermont towns; it does not certify that any specific town has been wronged, or that any specific allocation decision was incorrect. Individual town ranks should be read as approximate. The patterns across the full distribution are where the signal is clearest.

## Why the Money Flows the Way It Does

~~This is not a story about fraud or negligence. It is a story about structure.~~

The pattern appears to be driven less by individual decisions than by how the system itself is structured. 

Federal mitigation funding is, by design, heavily tied to disaster declarations. HMA grants become available in large quantities after a major disaster is declared, which means towns that were hit — and hit hard enough to generate a federal declaration — are positioned to apply. Towns that haven't yet experienced a declared disaster are not.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA funding obligated by year, showing spikes around 2011–2022 and 2023+, with Irene and 2023 events labeled]

There is also the question of capacity. HMA grants are not automatic. They require applications. They require matching funds, typically 25 percent of project costs. They require environmental reviews, benefit-cost analyses, and project scoping. For a Vermont town with a part-time administrator and a volunteer select board, this is a significant barrier. Larger towns, towns that have been through the process before, towns with established relationships with state hazard mitigation officers, have a structural advantage that compounds over time.

The data reflects this dynamic. Towns that received funding after Irene were significantly more likely to receive funding after the 2023 floods as well. Relationships built during one disaster cycle persist into the next. First-timers face a cold start.

[GRAPHIC: Bar chart — HMA project types by share of total funding, showing dominance of acquisition/buyouts]

The project type breakdown reveals another dimension. The single largest category of HMA spending in Vermont is **acquisition and buyouts**, purchasing flood-prone properties and removing them from the floodplain permanently. This is considered one of the most cost-effective mitigation strategies available, and there is real evidence it works. But it also means that **the primary mechanism for reducing flood losses is relocation**: families and businesses permanently displaced from the places they've lived, often with limited options for where to go next. For a town that hasn't yet been through a disaster declaration, that process hasn't started.

The communities waiting for the first declaration are often the ones least equipped to survive it.

It ranks in the 99th percentile of Vermont towns for vulnerability — driven by poverty rates, elderly population share, and limited transportation options that would leave many residents unable to evacuate quickly if they needed to. [GRAPHIC: Bar chart — number of zero-funding towns by need quintile, showing disproportionate concentration of high-need towns in the zero-funding bucket]

Across Vermont, the pattern is consistent. High-need towns are disproportionately represented among the towns that have never received mitigation funding. Of the 18 towns in the top 30 percent of the need index with zero HMA funding, many share common characteristics: smaller populations, lower median incomes, locations in areas that experienced significant but not federally declared flooding, or simply no prior engagement with the grant system. None of those characteristics make them less exposed to the next flood. They just make it harder to access the money designed to help them prepare.

## What Could Change — and What's Getting Harder

Vermont is in the middle of redrawing its Flood Insurance Rate Maps, the official federal designations that define which areas face high flood risk. The remapping is long overdue; the current maps in many areas date back to the 70's or 80's and do not reflect the changed hydrological reality of a warming, wetter climate. When the new maps are finalized, some towns will find themselves in higher-risk designations, a change that affects insurance requirements, property values, and eligibility for state programs including Vermont's Emergency Relief and Assistance Fund, covering up to [[92.5% of disaster funding](https://floodtraining.vermont.gov/protection-tools/get-ready-new-fema-flood-insurance-rate-maps)].

## A Note on the Data

The findings here are robust to methodology — the patterns hold across model specifications, normalization methods, and variable choices. But several important limitations apply.

- FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer mapping is incomplete across Vermont; some towns have partial or outdated coverage, which may affect their risk estimates.
- NFIP participation in Vermont is extremely low — fewer than 2 percent of housing units carry flood insurance — meaning insurance claims data significantly underrepresents actual flood losses statewide.
- Town-level aggregation obscures within-town variation. A high need score for Bennington doesn't mean every neighborhood faces the same exposure.
- Funding data reflects approved grants, not applications submitted or assistance requested and denied. The gap between what was applied for and what was approved is not captured here.

Individual town rankings should be read as approximate signals, not precise verdicts. The patterns in aggregate are where the analysis has the most confidence.

## The River Doesn't Wait

[PHOTO: Bennington streetscape or the Walloomsac River at current water level]

Vermont's flood maps are being redrawn. The federal program most directly aimed at the equity gap is in turmoil. In towns like Newport and Bennington, residents are already making decisions around flood risk: whether to stay, whether to elevate a home, whether insurance is affordable, whether the next storm will be worse. 

The funding system is not consistently reaching those communities before disaster strikes. 

Whether that changes is a political question, not a technical one. But any policy response starts with identifying the pattern clearly.
