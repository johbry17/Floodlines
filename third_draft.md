# Floodlines: The Geography of Institutional Memory

How the way institutions measure risk determines who receives protection — and why prior disaster experience often trumps future vulnerability.

## 1. Opening vignette

On lazy summer mornings the Walloomsac curls low through Bennington, threading under an old rail trestle and past houses with stoops just a few feet above the bank. In heavy weather the river remembers the same low yards, the same clogged culverts. By several technical measures Bennington is among Vermont’s most exposed towns. Yet the federal mitigation ledger — the Hazard Mitigation Assistance program meant to prevent future loss — shows no funding for the town.

A different scene sits on the northern edge of the state. Newport City hugs Lake Memphremagog with a picturesque waterfront and a population older and poorer than the statewide average. Its vulnerability score is among the highest in the state; its HMA line is near empty.

I use Bennington and Newport as recurring anchors because they make the statewide pattern concrete: places with geography, people, and histories you can point to on a map.

The pattern is simple to describe and hard to accept: federal mitigation dollars are better at remembering where big floods happened than at anticipating where future floods will cause the most harm. That matters because the practical question of adaptation is not just which models are most precise; it is which measurements and administrative choices steer resources toward people who need them.

This analysis is a prototype. It deliberately uses a compact, readable index to ask a single policy question that matters far beyond Vermont: how much precision do policymakers need before they can act meaningfully? The answer offered here is modest but hopeful — you do not need perfect models to make better decisions; you need usable indicators tied to smarter administration.

## 2. The core question

Why do some towns get mitigation funding while others do not? On the surface, the question sounds like a data problem: match exposure to dollars and you’re done. In practice the answer is institutional. Two forces determine who gets prioritized.

First: measurement. What counts as “need”? Do we measure expected damage, population vulnerability, both? The way we measure determines which places appear on a priority list.

Second: institutions. Programs have rules, timing, and paperwork. They reward towns with capacity — staff who can write grants, consultants who know the forms, and relationships with state and federal officers. They also reward towns that have already been through a disaster cycle.

Put together, measurement and institutional structure create what I call institutional geography: the uneven overlap of physical risk, local capacity, and the bureaucratic access that turns hazard into help. Measurement creates the map. Institutions decide which dots on that map get money.

This matters because a program that aims to prevent future harm should be forward‑looking. If instead it mostly funds places that already had detectable loss, then it is reinforcing a memory of the past rather than building protection for the future.

## 3. The floods and the system that follows them

Vermont’s memory of floods is recent and raw. In August 2011 Hurricane Irene stalled across the state and washed bridges, tore out roads, and filled basements. Twelve years later, in July 2023, a string of heavy storms again flooded downtowns and valley corridors. These events are anchors in public memory and in federal programming: declared disasters both unlock emergency relief and create the political moment for mitigation.

Mitigation through HMA is different from disaster relief. The work is slower and, in many ways, harder: acquisition and buyouts, culvert upgrades, elevation projects — they take time, planning, and money. HMA projects usually need a local match, environmental reviews, and benefit‑cost analyses. That requires people who can manage complexity.

After a big event, many towns find that momentum. State and federal teams arrive; consultants and engineers come in; grant writing begins. Those relationships and skills don’t disappear when the flood recedes. They persist. A town that navigated the process after Irene has a leg up on the next round of funding.

That accumulation of experience — the staff who stayed, the templates created, the political contacts made — is one of the ways memories become resources. It is also how the system amplifies prior winners.

Two program features are worth flagging. First, acquisition and buyouts are a major share of mitigation spending. They are effective but expensive and socially complicated; they tend to follow visible disaster narratives. Second, many federal levers are tied to disaster declarations. That link channels money to places with a record of big loss rather than to places with future projected harm.

## 4. The model in plain language: need = risk + vulnerability

To test whether mitigation follows future need, I built a simple prototype index. It has two parts.

Risk: a town’s modeled exposure to flood damage (measures like expected annual loss or FEMA’s composite risk score).

Vulnerability: a short set of socioeconomic indicators — poverty rate, share of elderly residents, and households without vehicle access — which help show how hard a community would be hit by similar damage.

Each component is converted into a percentile so towns are compared to one another rather than to absolute dollar figures. Funding is measured per resident and rescaled in the same way so we can compare need and funding on the same footing. The gap between the two — need minus funding — shows where a town appears underfunded relative to its modeled need.

This is intentionally not a sophisticated forecasting engine. It’s a readable, replicable ranking built to surface patterns, not to predict precise losses. That design is deliberate: policy often proceeds on limited, imperfect evidence. The question I wanted to probe was how much clarity a simple index could provide for decision‑makers.

Two practical points about the method: (1) using percentiles keeps the results communicable — a town in the 85th percentile is easier to explain than a raw z‑score — and (2) reducing skew in funding measures (by a log transform) makes comparisons more stable when a few towns have very large totals.

That’s it. Need = risk + vulnerability; compare that to rescaled funding; look for gaps. Simple, transparent, useful.

## 5. What the data shows (maps, scatterplots, quadrants)

Put the need index on a map and familiar patterns appear: valley corridors and town centers where built assets meet streams show up repeatedly. The Connecticut River corridor, the Winooski basin around Montpelier, the Battenkill and Walloomsac in the southwest, and pockets of the Northeast Kingdom are places where exposure and social vulnerability overlap.

Now layer on funding. The maps diverge. Towns with large HMA sums often match past disaster experience or administrative competence more than high modeled need. Several towns that rank very high on the need index have little or no HMA funding in their record — the recurring anchors Bennington and Newport are examples.

A scatterplot makes this clearer. Plot need rank on the x‑axis and funding rank on the y‑axis. If funding tracked need, the points would climb together. Instead the cloud is largely horizontal. Across model choices the correlation between need and funding ranks sits around 0.1–0.3 — weak. By contrast, the correlation between past NFIP claims paid and HMA funding is much stronger, roughly 0.5–0.6. In other words, prior claims are a better single predictor of mitigation awards than the forward‑looking composite.

A quadrant view helps make the pattern useful for policy: towns fall into categories — aligned (high need, high funding), underfunded (high need, low funding), overfunded (low need, high funding), low priority, and zero‑funded (no funding). Zero‑funded is notable: roughly half of Vermont’s towns have no recorded HMA funding, and a disproportionate share of high‑need towns are in that group.

The signal is not regionally narrow. It cuts across rural and small urban places, across the Northeast Kingdom and the southwest floodplains. The common factor is institutional: prior disaster experience and the administrative capacity to navigate grants.

These are patterns, not iron laws. But they point to a persistent tendency: the system rewards memory of past loss more reliably than projections of future harm.

## 6. Why the mismatch persists

Three interacting forces explain the pattern.

1) Disaster‑tied opportunity. Major mitigation dollars cluster after declared disasters. Declarations unlock money and political attention; they also create a clear narrative for buyouts and large projects. Places that have had big, declared events are naturally inline for mitigation.

2) Capacity and know‑how. HMA projects require staffing, technical studies, and a local match. Many small towns run emergency management on a part‑time basis. Towns that have recruited consultants or built relationships with state hazard officers can assemble competitive proposals more quickly.

3) Path dependence. Once a town learns the process, it is more likely to repeat it. Staff, templates, and interagency relationships persist. The system thus amplifies earlier successes: prior awards make future awards more likely.

There are subtler biases too. Benefit‑cost frameworks favor projects with quantifiable property benefits, which can disadvantage dispersed rural communities where human vulnerability is high but property density is low. Insurance claims act as an observable signal even when participation is low — a backward‑looking indicator that nudges funds toward places that have already submitted evidence of loss.

Taken together, these mechanisms create a system that reacts to the recent past more readily than it anticipates the near future. That tendency is not necessarily malicious; it is an emergent result of program design, rules, and limited local capacity. But from an equity and resilience perspective it matters: the most vulnerable places may be those least likely to have the institutional memory needed to unlock mitigation funds.

## 7. What might change — and what will be hard

Two shifts could rewire the system, but both face friction.

First: better maps. Many floodplain maps are being updated to reflect newer hydrology and more intense rainfall. New maps can change regulatory baselines and insurance requirements and, importantly, help target mitigation. But maps are slow, politically contested, and only useful if agencies use them to change eligibility and prioritization rules.

Second: smarter program design. Programs that lower match requirements, fund technical assistance for small towns, or create set‑asides for high‑vulnerability places can reduce the capacity gap. State programs that offer pre‑scoped projects or match funds can likewise help. These are practical levers: more staff, simpler application tracks, and proactive outreach can make a big difference.

The deeper work is institutional. Teaching a system to look forward means changing incentives and lowering transaction costs, not just changing models. It means funding the human side of mitigation: planners, grant writers, and regional teams that can carry towns through the process before disaster arrives.

A useful policy takeaway from the prototype: you do not need perfect models to act. A simple ranking that reliably flags high‑need towns — paired with targeted administrative support — could change funds distribution materially. Precision helps, but action depends more on institutional design than on the last decimal of model fit.

## 8. Ending

Bennington and Newport are reminders, not exceptions. The pattern this analysis reveals is not fate; it is a result of choices about what we measure and how we administer aid. We can change those choices.

This project is a prototype: a compact, readable way to surface institutional geography and start conversations about how mitigation dollars are targeted. The goal is modest and practical — make the invisible shape of institutional memory visible, then pair that signal with administrative reforms that let dollars get to places before they become emergencies.

That shift — from remembering the past to anticipating the future — is the practical heart of resilience.

*Methods note: the analysis combines modeled exposure (EAL/NRI variants) with a small vulnerability triad (poverty, elderly share, no‑vehicle households). Components are expressed as percentiles and combined into a simple need index. Funding is measured per resident and rescaled for comparison.
